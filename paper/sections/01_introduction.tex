\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A machine-learning model that ranks stocks by next-period return is judged by two different numbers. The first is an information coefficient: the cross-sectional correlation between its scores and realised returns. The second is the Sharpe ratio of a portfolio built from those scores after transaction costs. The literature on the first is large and mostly encouraging \citep{gkx2020,chenpelgerzhu2024,kellymalamudzhou2024}; the literature on the second is smaller and mostly discouraging \citep{novymarxvelikov2016,avramov2023}. The link between them is the fundamental law of active management \citep{grinold1989,clarke2002}: at fixed breadth, a Sharpe ratio is an information coefficient multiplied by a transfer coefficient that the portfolio construction determines, less a cost drag that turnover determines. This paper measures each term of that identity on one dataset, under a pre-registered protocol, and uses it to answer a question posed in operational form: can a model built from free data reach a net Sharpe ratio of 1.5 with a maximum drawdown below 25\%, and do graph methods help?

The dataset is deliberately modest. Prices come from a public survivor-only feed, fundamentals from SEC XBRL filings and earnings dates from 8-K item 2.02 filings, industry graphs from SIC codes and the BEA input--output tables, customer--supplier links from filing text. Nothing was purchased. That choice makes the exercise reproducible by a student and, as Section~\ref{sec:limits} discusses, imposes a survivorship bias that we bound but cannot remove \citep{shumway1997}. It also makes the result informative about the regime in which most independent research operates.

We make four contributions. (i)~We show that a pre-fixed shallow booster carries a cross-sectional rank signal whose sign replicates out of the window on which its configuration was chosen, on four later years and on one genuinely held-out year, and we document that per-refit hyperparameter selection on a noisy validation slice removes its advantage over a linear control. (ii)~We measure the transfer from information to return along a 55-cell grid of portfolio constructions (industry-neutral, volatility-scaled, beta-neutral, multi-horizon, partially adjusted) and show, by an exact cost-ladder identity, that the shortfall against the target is information rather than cost: no construction's gross Sharpe ratio reaches 1.5 on either window, so no cost level closes it, and the least demanding cell would need about $\numKappaLeastBoth\times$ its information coefficient. (iii)~We train relational graph networks end to end on four point-in-time firm graphs and compare them by paired $\Delta\IC$ with an identical network without a graph and, on the monthly and quarterly horizons, with the same network trained on degree-preserving rewired graphs \citep{maslovsneppen2002}; the paired $\Delta\IC$ against the no-graph baseline is indistinguishable from zero on every horizon, and the rewired-graph comparison, completed only as IC levels, points the same way. (iv)~We compute the forward sample needed to confirm the signal at its own noise level and show that a Sharpe ratio of 0.3 would need decades; the sizing decision therefore rests on a prior.

Related work. \citet{gkx2020} established trees and shallow networks as the strongest cross-sectional predictors on CRSP, with small out-of-sample $R^2$ and economically large decile spreads before cost; \citet{avramov2023} showed that much of that spread sits in microcaps and in high-turnover signals that costs remove. Economic links predict returns at the firm level \citep{cohenfrazzini2008,menzlyozbas2010}, which motivates graphs. Graph neural networks \citep{kipfwelling2017,hamilton2017,velickovic2018,schlichtkrull2018} are the natural end-to-end form of that idea; we know of few tests of them against a rewired-graph null on a point-in-time equity panel; ours is partial (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). Inference under selection follows \citet{harveyliuzhu2016}, \citet{baileylopez2014} and \citet{hansen2005}; Sharpe-ratio uncertainty follows \citet{lo2002} and \citet{ledoitwolf2008}; the decay and fragility of published anomalies \citep{mcleanpontiff2016,houxuezhang2020} is the prior against which a small signal should be read.
