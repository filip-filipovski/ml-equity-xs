\section{Power and the sizing decision}\label{sec:power}

A signal this small raises a practical question: how much forward data would confirm it at its own level? We answer it from the model's own monthly IC series over 2009--2022 (168 months; mean $\numICMeanTwentyTwo$, standard deviation $\numICSdTwentyTwo$, lag-one autocorrelation $\numICLagTwentyTwo$) and from the Sharpe-ratio standard error of eq.~\eqref{eq:se}. For a one-sided 5\% test of $H_0$: mean $\IC\le0$, the analytic sample for 80\% power at a true IC of 0.03 is $\numMonthsICThree$ months; to tell the 2019--2023 level (0.06) from the long-run level needs at least as many (a lower bound, since the long-run level is itself estimated); at a true IC of 0.06 the test needs $\numMonthsICSix$ months; six months of forward data have power $\numPowerSixMonthsICThree\pct$ and $\numPowerSixMonthsICSix\pct$ at the two levels. A block-bootstrap version of the same calculation (Table~\ref{tab:power}) agrees. For a book, confirming a Sharpe ratio of 0.3 against zero takes about $\numYearsSharpeThree$ years of monthly data and a Sharpe ratio of 1.0 about $\numYearsSharpeOne$ years (Figure~\ref{fig:power}).

The consequence is not that the signal is absent; it is that the property cannot be confirmed at its own Sharpe level on any horizon that matters for an allocation decision. A single strong year, such as 2023, moves the posterior mean by little against fourteen years whose books earn a Sharpe ratio of 0.1--0.2 (the monthly decile book $\numDecileSharpe$, $\numDecileAnn\pct$ per year, $\MaxDD$ $\numDecileDD\pct$, over 2009--2022). Sizing such a signal is therefore a prior decision about how much of a small, replicated but unconfirmed property one is willing to hold, not an estimate the data can deliver.
